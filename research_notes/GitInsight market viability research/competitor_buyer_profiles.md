# Competitor Buyer Profiles: Software Engineering Intelligence (SEI) Platforms in 2026

Companies covered: LinearB, Swarmia, Jellyfish, Faros AI, Waydev, Axify, DX (getdx.com)

## Do any of these 7 companies have a genuine self-serve or free tier usable by a team of 2-15 engineers, or do they all require a sales conversation / minimum team size?

### Takeaway
Only Swarmia and Waydev have a genuine, currently-live free tier that a 2-15 engineer team could use unassisted; LinearB removed its free tier in its current pricing structure and now gates entry behind a 50-developer minimum; Jellyfish, Faros AI, and DX are fully sales-gated with no public self-serve option at any size; Axify has no free tier but offers a 14-day full-access trial with no minimum size stated.

### Cited Findings
- **Swarmia**: Free plan is explicitly capped at "companies with up to 9 developers," giving Standard-plan features minus HRIS integrations and Swarmia AI — this is the only clearly-stated free tier among the seven that is scoped exactly to a 2-15 engineer team. — [Swarmia Pricing](https://www.swarmia.com/pricing/)
- **Swarmia** paid Standard plan is $45/developer/month billed annually ($42/dev EUR in some regions); Enterprise is $55/developer/month with on-prem integrations, HR system integrations, and professional services; à la carte modules (AI adoption tracking $5/dev/mo, developer surveys $9/dev/mo, software capitalization $18/dev/mo, productivity & AI impact $23/dev/mo) can be bought individually. — [Swarmia Pricing](https://www.swarmia.com/pricing/)
- **Swarmia** offers a 50% startup discount for companies under 50 developers and 30% for companies with 50+ developers in their first year via partner programs — implying their "normal" customer is assumed to be 50+ developers even though the free tier technically supports much smaller teams. — [Swarmia Pricing](https://www.swarmia.com/pricing/)
- **Waydev**: Has a "Startup (Free)" plan — $0, no credit card required, covering 10 repositories and 3 months of historical data, explicitly framed as "the free way to see whether AI is improving." This is usable by a small team. — [Waydev Pricing](https://waydev.co/pricing/)
- **Waydev** paid tiers: Pro at $29/contributor/month (~$17,400/year for 50 engineers), Premium at $49/contributor/month (~$29,400/year for 50 engineers); Enterprise is custom/unlimited. Managers and executives get free "operational user" seats on all paid tiers (5 free on Pro, 20 free on Premium, unlimited on Enterprise). — [Waydev Pricing](https://waydev.co/pricing/)
- **LinearB**: Current pricing page (linearb.io/pricing, fetched September 2026) shows no permanent free tier — instead a "45-day free trial, with no commitment or payment method required," after which customers must convert to a paid plan. The paid tiers have explicit team-size floors: Essentials at $29/contributor/month is "Built for engineering teams with 50+ developers" (GitHub Cloud only), and Enterprise at $59/contributor/month is "Built for engineering teams with 100+ developers." Both tiers bill for every developer added to a team regardless of activity level. — [LinearB Pricing](https://linearb.io/pricing)
- Note: Third-party aggregator sites (Vendr, Tekpon) describe an older/different LinearB structure with an 8-10 developer free tier and a $30/dev "Business" tier — this conflicts with the current linearb.io/pricing page, suggesting LinearB has recently (2025-2026) repositioned upmarket and eliminated its small-team free tier. The primary source (linearb.io itself) should be treated as authoritative over the aggregator summaries. — [Vendr LinearB](https://www.vendr.com/marketplace/linearb); contradicted by [LinearB Pricing](https://linearb.io/pricing)
- **Axify**: No free tier, but "every paid plan includes a 14-day free trial with full access" (credit card required, cancellable before day 14 with no charge). Starter plan is a flat $100/month (unlimited contributors); Pro is $29/active-contributor/month (annual billing only); Enterprise is custom/volume-discounted. Axify defines "active contributor" narrowly (a commit, PR, or Jira ticket move in the last 30 days), so inactive people aren't billed — this is the most small-team-friendly paid pricing structure of the group, and the flat $100/mo Starter tier has no stated minimum team size. — [Axify Pricing](https://axify.io/pricing)
- **Jellyfish**: Pricing page (jellyfish.co/pricing) contains no tiers, no per-seat figures, and no free tier — the only call to action is "Request a Quote"/"Talk to Sales." Pricing is "based on number of seats and specific modules selected." — [Jellyfish Pricing](https://jellyfish.co/pricing/)
- Third-party data (Vendr) puts Jellyfish's median annual contract value at $35,920 across 91 recorded purchases, with real deals starting around $16,500 as of July 2026 — implying a de facto minimum deal size even though no minimum team size is publicly stated. — [CodePulse: Jellyfish Pricing 2026](https://codepulsehq.com/guides/jellyfish-pricing-review)
- **Faros AI**: No public tiers or free tier; uses "a consumption-based pricing model" and states "specific pricing details are not publicly documented; contact Faros for a tailored quote." One secondary source cites an entry SaaS price of "$29 per contributor per month" and estimates $3,000-$5,000/month for a 100-developer org running AI Impact + DORA modules, plus potential $40-100k partner-led implementation costs on top of the license — but this figure was not confirmed on Faros's own pricing page. — [Faros Pricing](https://www.faros.ai/pricing); entry price cited by [CodePulse: Faros AI Pricing 2026](https://codepulsehq.com/guides/faros-ai-pricing-review)
- **Faros AI**'s own pricing page explicitly states: "Small teams with minimal AI usage may not realize the full value of Faros's advanced features," and it contrasts itself with competitor Opsera, calling Opsera "SMB-only" — implying Faros positions itself as deliberately not-for-SMB. — [Faros Pricing](https://www.faros.ai/pricing)
- **DX (getdx.com)**: Pricing page (getdx.com/pricing) contains no published tiers, per-developer costs, free tier, or trial information — only a "Get a demo" CTA. — [DX Pricing](https://getdx.com/pricing/)
- One secondary source claims a "$15,000/year minimum" tied to "a 15-person team requirement," but this was not verified on DX's own site and should be treated as unconfirmed. — [Exceeds AI: GetDX Enterprise Pricing Guide](https://blog.exceeds.ai/getdx-enterprise-pricing-guide/)
- Vendr's 2026 data on DX shows a median annual contract value of $53,760 across 112 recorded purchases — the highest median ACV of any company in this set with third-party pricing data — with average negotiated savings of 26.96%, implying heavy sales-led enterprise negotiation is standard. — [WebSearch result citing Vendr DX data, no direct URL captured in this pass]
- DX also offers "no-cost proof of concepts for a subset of your organization" as a way to validate impact pre-purchase — but this is explicitly framed as a sales-assisted pilot, not self-serve. — [DX Why DX](https://getdx.com/why-dx/)

### Inferences
- There is a clear split: Swarmia and Waydev serve the "small team, self-serve, try before you buy" segment; LinearB has apparently exited that segment as of 2026 (removing its old ~8-10 dev free tier in favor of a hard 50-developer floor); Jellyfish, Faros AI, and DX never served that segment and remain fully sales-gated regardless of team size.
- Axify's flat-fee Starter tier ($100/mo unlimited contributors, no stated minimum) is arguably the most accessible paid option for a 2-15 engineer team among all seven, even without a permanent free tier, because of its low flat price and lack of per-seat billing at that tier.
- The specific direction of change (LinearB's apparent removal of a small-team free plan) suggests a broader industry trend of "moving upmarket" away from small teams by 2026, consistent with Faros AI and DX's explicit enterprise-only stance.

### Gaps
- Could not verify LinearB's historical free-tier terms directly (only via conflicting secondary sources); the exact date LinearB removed/changed its free tier is unknown.
- DX's exact minimum contract size and team-size floor are not confirmed on getdx.com itself — only via a secondary blog (Exceeds AI) whose reliability as a primary source is unverified.
- Faros AI's actual per-contributor starting price ($29) is sourced from a third-party aggregator (CodePulse), not confirmed on faros.ai/pricing itself, which discloses no numbers at all.

## Is there a clear size cliff below which none of these companies want a customer (e.g. "we don't take teams under 30 engineers")?

### Takeaway
Yes — LinearB has the most explicit, numeric size cliff (50 developers minimum for its cheapest paid tier, 100+ for Enterprise), and Faros AI states outright that small teams "may not realize the full value" of its product; Jellyfish and DX don't publish a number but their sales-gated, no-free-tier model combined with $35K-$54K median annual contract values functions as a de facto cliff far above 15 engineers; Swarmia, Waydev, and Axify are the exceptions with no stated hard floor.

### Cited Findings
- LinearB Essentials tier: explicitly "Built for engineering teams with 50+ developers"; Enterprise tier: explicitly "Built for engineering teams with 100+ developers." Both are minimum billable-user thresholds, not just suggestions — LinearB bills for every developer added to a team regardless of whether they're a target segment. — [LinearB Pricing](https://linearb.io/pricing)
- Faros AI pricing page states: "Small teams with minimal AI usage may not realize the full value of Faros's advanced features," directly discouraging small-team adoption. A secondary source characterizes the "honest Faros buyer profile" as "500+ engineers, internal data team of 5+, custom-metric culture, enterprise compliance with audit-trail requirements, and the budget already exists" — this specific figure (500+) is from a third-party blog, not Faros's own marketing copy, and should be treated as an inference rather than a confirmed Faros statement. — [Faros Pricing](https://www.faros.ai/pricing); 500+ figure from [CodePulse: Faros AI Pricing 2026](https://codepulsehq.com/guides/faros-ai-pricing-review)
- DX's benchmark segments are built around "100-500 engineers" and "500+ engineers" cohorts, according to a search-result summary of getdx.com/blog/expanded-benchmark-segments — suggesting DX's own analytics/benchmarking products are designed with three-digit-plus engineering orgs in mind, even if no hard minimum is stated for purchasing itself. — [DX Expanded Benchmark Segments](https://getdx.com/blog/expanded-benchmark-segments/)
- Swarmia's startup-discount structure (50% off under 50 developers, 30% off at 50+ in year one) implies no hard cliff — the free tier and discounts are explicitly built to accommodate teams smaller than 50 — but the existence of a "under 50 developers" discount bracket at all suggests Swarmia's internal expectation is that most paying customers exceed 50 developers. — [Swarmia Pricing](https://www.swarmia.com/pricing/)
- Waydev and Axify state no explicit minimum team size anywhere in their pricing pages; both price purely per-active-contributor (Waydev) or per-active-contributor/flat-fee (Axify) with no stated floor. — [Waydev Pricing](https://waydev.co/pricing/); [Axify Pricing](https://axify.io/pricing)
- Jellyfish and Faros AI's pricing pages both route every visitor to "Request a Quote"/"contact sales" with zero public figures, which functions as a soft, opaque cliff: a company evaluating self-serve for a 2-15 person team would find no entry point at all, regardless of whether Jellyfish would technically sell to them. — [Jellyfish Pricing](https://jellyfish.co/pricing/); [Faros Pricing](https://www.faros.ai/pricing)

### Inferences
- The clearest, most quantified "cliff" in the entire set is LinearB's 50-developer floor for its cheapest tier — this is the only company among the seven with an explicit numeric minimum stated on its own pricing page as of this research (September 2026).
- Faros AI and DX behave as though they have a cliff around several hundred engineers (based on benchmark segments and buyer-profile language) but do not publish an exact number — their gatekeeping is done through sales qualification rather than published tiers.
- Jellyfish's lack of any published tiers combined with a ~$35,920 median ACV suggests an effective floor in the tens of thousands of dollars per year, which would be uneconomical for a 2-15 engineer team even if Jellyfish's sales team would technically take the deal.

### Gaps
- No source directly quotes Jellyfish, Faros AI, or DX sales/BDR scripts or sales-qualification criteria (e.g., "we require at least X engineers to have a call") — this would require access to sales call transcripts, SDR outreach playbooks, or direct interviews with sales reps, none of which turned up in public web search.
- No confirmation of whether Jellyfish, Faros AI, or DX would turn away a genuinely small team (e.g., 10 engineers) that had budget — the "cliff" for these three is inferred from pricing opacity and stated buyer-profile characteristics, not a stated refusal policy.

## Who is the internal champion that drives adoption — is it always a manager/VP evaluating team performance, or are individual engineers ever the buyer?

### Takeaway
Across all seven companies, marketing and buying-committee evidence consistently points to engineering leadership (VP Engineering, CTO, Director of Engineering, Engineering Manager) as the champion/buyer, sometimes joined by Finance/CFO stakeholders for capitalization or R&D-tax-credit use cases; individual engineers are consistently framed as end users/stakeholders whose trust is needed, not as the purchasing decision-maker, in any of the seven company's own marketing.

### Cited Findings
- A third-party buyer's guide (Swarmia's own blog) states the purchasing dynamic explicitly: "You need engineers to trust it, managers to champion it, and executives to support it" — placing "champion" status specifically with managers, not individual engineers, and separating "trust" (engineers) from "champion" (managers) and "support" (executives) as three distinct roles in the buying process. — [Swarmia Buyer's Guide](https://www.swarmia.com/blog/buyers-guide-engineering-intelligence-platforms/)
- The same Swarmia buyer's guide content, when fetched, lists five distinct stakeholder types in the decision: Engineering Leaders (organizational visibility/strategic insights), Engineering Managers (team-level bottleneck identification), Software Engineers (workflow integration/daily usability — evaluators, not buyers), Product Managers (delivery timeline predictions), and Finance Teams (audit-ready reporting) — explicitly describing this as "an organizational decision rather than individual contributor-driven." — [Swarmia Buyer's Guide](https://www.swarmia.com/blog/buyers-guide-engineering-intelligence-platforms/)
- Waydev's own marketing states the platform is "most used by engineering leaders, CTOs, and development managers who need visibility into team performance, delivery pace, and workflow bottlenecks, while founders, CEOs, and other executives use it to monitor engineering output and planning" — no mention of individual engineers as buyers, only as subjects of the data. — [WebSearch summary of Waydev marketing, primary source: waydev.co]
- Waydev's pricing structure materially reinforces this: "operational users" (defined as managers/executives, not code-committing engineers) get free seats on every paid tier (5 free on Pro, 20 free on Premium, unlimited on Enterprise) while contributors (engineers who commit code) are the metered/billed unit — meaning the product's own cost structure treats managers as non-paying observers and engineers as the "product" being measured and billed for, not as customers evaluating the tool for themselves. — [Waydev Pricing](https://waydev.co/pricing/)
- Jellyfish's pricing page frames its solutions around "Engineering executives, Engineering managers, Platform engineering teams, Product leaders, Finance teams, Software developers" — the ordering (executives and managers listed before developers) and the explicit inclusion of Finance as a persona for DevFinOps/R&D tax credit modules signals that CFO/Finance stakeholders are often co-buyers alongside engineering leadership for Jellyfish specifically, driven by its tax-credit/capitalization use case. — [Jellyfish Pricing](https://jellyfish.co/pricing/)
- A secondary source characterizes one of these platforms (unspecified but implied to be one of the DevFinOps-style tools) as "Best for: VPs of Engineering who need to present R&D spend and capitalization data to a CFO or board on a recurring basis" — reinforcing VP Engineering as the champion who must in turn justify the purchase to a CFO/board audience. — [WebSearch result, buyer's guide aggregator, exact source URL not individually captured]
- Faros AI's own pricing-page buyer profile explicitly names "engineering leaders, compliance stakeholders, and resource-constrained teams" — again leadership and compliance roles, not individual contributors, as the target audience. — [Faros Pricing](https://www.faros.ai/pricing)
- Axify's stated target roles are "Engineering managers, engineering leaders, product leaders, engineering & product operations professionals" — consistent with the pattern; Axify also maintains role-specific marketing pages ("Axify for Engineering Managers," "Axify for Software Engineering and Product Operations Professionals"), reinforcing that its go-to-market is organized around management/operations roles rather than individual engineers. — [Axify for Engineering Managers](https://axify.io/roles/engineering-managers); [Axify for Eng & Product Ops](https://axify.io/roles/software-engineering-product-operations-professionals)
- DX's own site describes serving "every level of an engineering organization—from CXOs to platform teams to frontline managers," according to search-result summaries of getdx.com — explicitly naming CXOs and platform teams (not individual engineers) alongside frontline managers as its constituency. — [DX site content via WebSearch summary, primary source getdx.com]
- General industry summary (aggregated from multiple buyer's-guide sources): "The buying committee typically includes VP Engineering, CTO, CFO, and sometimes a Chief of Staff" for engineering intelligence platform purchases. — [Uplevel: A Buyer's Guide to Engineering Intelligence Tools 2026](https://uplevelteam.com/blog/engineering-intelligence-tools-buyers-guide)

### Inferences
- None of the seven companies' own marketing materials position an individual engineer (a non-manager, non-lead individual contributor) as the buyer or champion anywhere in the research gathered; the consistent pattern is VP Engineering / CTO / Director / Engineering Manager as champion, with CFO/Finance as a secondary co-buyer specifically for capitalization/tax-credit-oriented offerings (notably Jellyfish's DevFinOps module), and individual engineers cast only as "users who must trust/adopt it" — a constituency to be won over, not a purchasing authority.
- Waydev's "operational users free, contributors billed" pricing mechanic is a particularly concrete artifact of this pattern: it structurally encodes that managers/executives are the platform's customers (free access) while engineers are the subject of measurement (the billed unit), which is a strong practical signal about who the buyer actually is, independent of any marketing copy.
- Platform/DevEx teams are mentioned (DX, Jellyfish) as a distinct persona alongside VP Engineering — suggesting that at larger organizations, a dedicated "Platform Engineering" or "Developer Experience" team can be the operational champion/owner of the tool, even though the budget sign-off likely still sits with a VP/CTO/Finance layer above them.

### Gaps
- No source found that identifies a case of an individual engineer (not in a management/lead/platform-team role) independently purchasing or expensing one of these seven tools — this may simply not occur, or may occur but not be documented in public marketing/press material, which is inherently biased toward describing top-down enterprise sales motions.
- Could not find direct interview or first-person testimony (e.g., a champion's own account of "how I convinced my company to buy X") for any of the seven companies specifically in 2025-2026; findings here are inferred from each company's own persona/marketing language and third-party buyer's-guide summaries rather than direct case studies.
- LinearB's specific champion/buyer persona language was not directly captured in this research pass (searches focused on pricing for LinearB); its marketing-stated ideal buyer role is a gap that a follow-up query to linearb.io's own "solutions" or "customers" pages could fill.
